Design philosophy

The Oxide Doctrine

I

Iron remembers.

Recording began when someone realized rust could hold a voice. An instrument's first duty is memory — to keep what your hands did at 2 a.m. so the morning can judge it fairly. Nothing you play into a Ferric machine evaporates.

II

Patina over polish.

A Land Rover looks better at forty than at four. Software rarely does — because it's built to be replaced, not kept. We build the other way: interfaces that feel used-in, not used up. Wear is a record of work.

III

The limit is the instrument.

Sixteen pads. Four tracks. One reel. A constraint is not a missing feature — it's the thing that forces a decision, and decisions are what finished songs are made of. Infinite tracks have finished nothing.

IV

Night work.

These machines are built for the bench after hours — headphones on, house dark, one lit pad glowing. Cold panel, warm signal. The panel stays out of your way; the warmth comes from what you make on it.

The hallmark

Struck, not printed

Iron is element 26. Ferric was founded in '26. The hallmark — Fe over 26, ringed in oxide — is our maker's stamp, borrowed from the old practice of striking silver with an assay mark so anyone, in any century, could tell who made it and whether it was honest metal.

The model number is always larger than the maker's mark. Ferric signs; it never headlines.

On every instrument the stamp sits small in the corner of the faceplate, its signal cell lit in that machine's own color — amber on the P–16, tape red on the T–4. One mark, worn differently by each machine.
