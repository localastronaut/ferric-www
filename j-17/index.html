01Why it exists

A replica alone is a museum piece.

J–17 started as a JU-06A replica and kept the bones: one page of faders, DCO into filter into amp, four voices with one envelope each, the chorus where the Juno sound lives. No menus, no settings page. What stays from 1984 stays because it works.

What's new is the answer to the museum problem. A Juno's oscillator is fixed forever; here it's whatever you recorded this morning. Import a voice memo, a door hinge, a street — the app listens first, tunes the keyboard to it, takes the attack into the envelope, and the splice arrives sounding like itself. The whole 1984 panel still applies on top.

One-of-a-kind sounds come out of recordings only you have. Two people running the same synth should not arrive at the same record.

“Every voice drifts ±1.8 cents — the vintage is built in, not a switch.”

03The field manual

Splice. Shape. Pattern. Tape.

The splice

The second source. Import any recording, set IN and OUT, commit. The app listens first — it hears the recording's pitch and tunes the keyboard to it. Loop rolls a window you dial in by ear; the seam never clicks. WOW is tape wander, GRIT is the old-sampler crush, WANDER sends the loop walking.
A → B — THE LOOP WINDOW. HOLD A KEY, DRAG BY EAR.

The panel

Vertical faders with 0–100 readouts that match the printed patch sheet. 24 dB lowpass — the voice of the instrument; RES rings the edge to the point of scream. HPF step 0 adds the low boost — the Juno bass secret. Chorus I is slow and wide, II faster and deeper, I+II the big wash. Off is honest mono.
DCO→VCF→VCA→HPF→CHORUS

The pattern

The family's own grid, wired for notes — learn it on the drums, play it here. Rows are keys, columns are 16ths. Four clicks count you in; quantize is past-biased, so running late never throws a hit ahead. It loops notes, not audio — change the patch and the pattern changes with it.
ROWS ARE KEYS · HELD A BAR, WRITTEN A BAR

The hardware

Wire a JU-06A in and its sliders drive this panel 1:1, no mapping — two panels, one sound. The paper patch sheet prints every fader 0–100 so a sound made here can be dialed into the real hardware by hand. The phone loves the synth back.

04How it's used

Patch to pattern to tape

  1. PATCHStep through your sounds, then the factory dozen. The dot on the LCD means edited since load.
  2. PLAYThirteen keys upright, twenty-five sideways, multi-touch with glissando — or any MIDI keyboard, or the JU itself.
  3. SHAPEDrag the faders; tap a section title and it opens big with the keys still under your hands, so you hear every move.
  4. PATTERNEight slots, one to eight bars, count-in of four. LINK plays the filled ones in order as a song.
  5. KEEPThe tape is always rolling the last four minutes. One button exports the WAV, the MIDI line, and the patch sidecar — who · what · when.
05In the kit

The kit's keyboard voice

THE CLOCK

Set EXT and J–17 falls in step with P–16's or T–6's clock; Ableton Link locks it to everything on the network. It keeps sounding in the background, mixing with the rest of the kit.

THE CRATE

The deck's crate key drops a cut straight onto T–6's FROM J–17 shelf. On the tape machine, J–17 also mounts as a patchbay instrument — the tape prints the clean digital out, no mic, no bleed.

THE SHELF

Name a sound and it's a file — AirDrop it, back it up, put it on the library's PATCHES shelf. L–1 can hand it back to the synth from any device. Loading is not keeping; SAVE is how it becomes yours.

THE REFEREE

The C labels name the sounding octave, range included — what S–7 would read. If the synth and the tuner disagree about pitch, the synth is wrong.

Also an Audio Unit — aumu · j17i — the full panel in the plugin window; move a fader in the app and a live AU retunes mid-note.
